## Digital-to-analog conversion (2)

- Converting digital data into a band-pass analog signal is called digital-to-analog conversion.
- Converting a low-pass analog signal into a band-pass analog signal is called analog-to-analog conversion.
- Digital-to-analog conversion is the process of varying one of the characteristics of an analog signal according to the digital data.

## Digital-to-analog conversion (3)

- A sine wave is defined by its amplitude, frequency, and phase.
- There are 4 modulation methods: amplitude shift keying (ASK), frequency shift keying (FSK), phase shift keying (PSK), and quadrature amplitude modulation (QAM). The most widely used is QAM.

## Digital-to-analog conversion (5)

- A data element is the smallest unit of information (a bit). A signal element is the smallest constant signal.
- The carrier signal is the high-frequency signal generated by the sending device.
- Bandwidth is directly proportional to the signal rate
  - Except for FSK
- Example: Bit rate is 8000 bps. Baud rate is 1000 baud. How many bits are there in each signal element? How many different signal elements are needed?
  - r = N/S = 8000/1000 = 8
  - r = log₂L → L = 256

## ASK – Amplitude Shift Keying

- The amplitude of the carrier signal is varied. Many amplitude levels can be created.
- Usually a two-level variation is used, with one of the levels being 0 (on-off keying).
- BW is at least S and at most 2S. 0<d<1; modulation depends on the filtering process.

## ASK – Amplitude Shift Keying (3)

- If the digital data is expressed in unipolar NRZ, ASK is produced by multiplying the carrier signal by the NRZ signal.
- Instead of two levels, 4, 8, or more levels can be used. The bandwidth required for full-duplex ASK increases because a separate carrier is used in each direction.

## ASK – Amplitude Shift Keying (4)

- It is affected by sudden changes in power
- It is a simple but inefficient modulation technique
- On voice-grade lines, it is used at data rates up to 1200 bps
- It can be used to transmit digital data over optical fiber

## FSK - Frequency Shift Keying

- The frequency of the carrier signal is varied.
- With binary FSK, signals at two different frequencies are used.

## Binary Frequency-Shift Keying (BFSK)

- Fewer errors occur than with ASK
- On voice-grade lines, it is used at data rates up to 1200 bps
- It can be used for high-frequency (3 to 30 MHz) radio communication
- It can be used at higher frequencies on LANs that use coaxial cable

## Multiple Frequency-Shift Keying (MFSK)
- More than two frequencies are used
- It is more bandwidth-efficient but more prone to errors. s_i(t) = A cos(2π f_i t), 1 ≤ i ≤ M
- f_i = f_c + (2i – 1 – M) f_d
- f_c = carrier frequency
- f_d = difference frequency
- M = number of signal levels = 2^L
- L = number of bits per signal element

## Multiple Frequency-Shift Keying (MFSK) (3)
- To match the input data rate, each output signal element is held for a duration Ts: Ts = LT seconds (T is the bit period, data rate = 1/T).
- One signal element encodes L bits of input data

## Multiple Frequency-Shift Keying (MFSK) (4)
- Total bandwidth is 2Mfd
- Minimum required frequency separation: 2fd = 1/Ts
- Therefore, the bandwidth required for the modulator is Wd = 2Mfd = M/Ts
- The bandwidth required for FSK depends on the distance between the carrier frequencies

## Example

- Bit rate is 3 Mbps. It is desired to send 3 bits at a time. Carrier frequency is 10 MHz. How many levels are required, what is the baud rate, and what is the bandwidth?
  - M = 2³ = 8
  - S = 3000/3 = 1000 baud
  - The minimum spacing must be S: 2fd = 1000, B = 2Mfd = 8x1000 = 8000

## PSK – Phase Shift Keying

- The phase of the carrier signal is varied.
- BPSK uses two signals with phases that differ by 0° and 180°.
- PSK is less affected by noise than ASK.
- PSK requires only one carrier frequency, whereas FSK requires as many as the number of levels.

## Two-level PSK

- The phase of the carrier signal is shifted to represent the data. The phase shift is relative to a fixed reference signal.

## Phase-Shift Keying (PSK) - Differential PSK

- Differential PSK
  - The phase shift is made relative to the previously transmitted bit, not to a reference signal
  - It does not require a timing mechanism

## Phase-Shift Keying (PSK) - DPSK

- Differential PSK (DPSK)
  - Shifts the phase relative to the previous bit
- Binary 0 – send a signal with the same phase as the previous bit
- Binary 1 – send a signal with the opposite phase of the previous bit

## QPSK – Quadrature Phase Shift Keying

- Two bits are sent with one signal.
- Each of the two bits is processed by a separate multiplier.
- The two signals are added, producing a signal with phases of 45°, -45°, 135°, -135°.

## Analog-to-analog conversion

- Analog information is represented by an analog signal.
- Analog-to-analog conversion is needed if the medium is band-pass.
- Analog-to-analog conversion is performed with amplitude modulation (AM), frequency modulation (FM), and phase modulation (PM).

## Amplitude Modulation

- The amplitude of the carrier signal is varied.

## Amplitude Modulation (2)

- BW is twice that of the modulating signal.
- AM radio channels have a BW of 10 kHz.
- To avoid interference, a 10 kHz guard band is placed between each pair of adjacent channels.
- AM carrier frequencies are between 530 and 1700 kHz.

## Frequency Modulation

- The frequency of the carrier signal is varied.
- BW is 2(1+ β)B. β is the modulation index and is usually taken as 4.

## Frequency Modulation (2)

- FM radio channels have a BW of 200 kHz.
- FM carrier frequencies are between 88 and 108 MHz.

## Phase Modulation

- The phase of the carrier signal is varied according to the amplitude of the modulating signal.
- The BW for PM is the same as for FM.
