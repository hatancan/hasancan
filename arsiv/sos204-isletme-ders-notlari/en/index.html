SOS204 Business: Summary of Course Slides, Weeks 1–13 (Business, Management, Production, Marketing, Human Resources, Finance)

SOS204 – İşletme · 01.03.2015 · 14.11.2026

The lecture slides of the Başkent University SOS204 Business course for weeks 1 through 13 (the week 8 file is missing from the archive). The slides do not name an author; from the phrase "Instructor: Dr. F. Bahar Işın" in the student presentations, it appears that this person is the course's instructor. All the slides and a "combined course summary" document are available as PDFs in the ZIP file at the end of the post. The summary below gives the main headings and key definitions by week; details are in the slides.

Week 1 – Introduction: Need, Production, and the Business

  • Need: a lack arising from the person themselves or from social relationships; it is divided into primary (physiological) and secondary (arising from social relationships and duties). Maslow's hierarchy of needs: physiological, safety, social, esteem, and self-actualization needs.
  • Demand: the expressed form of a want, backed by purchasing power, directed at the product expected to provide the greatest benefit.
  • Production: all activities carried out to bring economic goods and services into being; it creates form, place, time, ownership (exchange), and information utility.
  • Business: an organization that produces and/or markets economic goods and services by bringing production factors together in a harmonious way to meet people's needs. Its three basic characteristics: creating utility, bringing production factors together harmoniously, and earning a profit.
  • The functional structure of a modern business: the general function is management; the basic functions are production and marketing; the support functions are finance and human resources; "management + production + marketing + finance + human resources" are the 5 basic functions of a business.
  • Tips for active listening are also covered, along with the "Red Queen" analogy for the need to keep running continuously in competition.

Week 2 – Objectives of the Business

General objectives: (long-term) earning profit, providing social benefit, and ensuring the continuity of the business; the balance between the first two secures the third. Profit is the net surplus obtained by subtracting total expenses from total revenue; it serves functions such as a symbol of success, a means of measurement and control, a source of self-financing, a means of motivating employees, and a means of reputation. The social benefit objective covers environmental sensitivity, efficient use of resources, respect for competition and ethical rules, meeting employee expectations, and fulfilling responsibilities toward the state. Reasons for social responsibility awareness: professional managers, the growth of businesses and their having many shareholders, increasing competition, image, globalization, and the total quality system.

Week 3 – The Business System and Its Environment

The business's internal, micro, macro, and international environments. A system is a whole whose parts are interrelated; it has four elements: input (natural, human, capital, and information resources), process, output (goods and services), and feedback/control. The business is a dynamic system; it continuously assesses its environment in order to exploit opportunities and take precautions against threats. Macro environment: demographic, cultural and social, economic (and so on) environments.

Week 4 – Types of Business

According to the commercial code, private businesses are divided legally into four: sole proprietorships, partnerships (ordinary partnership; commercial partnerships: personal partnerships – general and limited; capital partnerships – limited partnership with capital divided into shares, joint-stock, and limited liability), cooperatives, and association and foundation enterprises. Advantages of sole proprietorships: easy to establish, flexible structure, no profit sharing, and authority concentrated in one hand; disadvantages: limited size, the life of the business being bound to the life of its owner, and unlimited liability. In a general partnership, the partners have unlimited liability for the company's debts; in a limited partnership, the general partners (komandite) have unlimited liability, while the limited partners (komanditer) are liable only up to the capital they have contributed (the limited partner has no right to manage, object, or represent, and may transfer their share).

Week 5 – Establishment, Investment Decisions, and Capital (5-1, 5-2, 5-3)

  • Reasons for starting a business: expectation of profit/earnings, establishing a business useful to society, starting and running an independent business, assets acquired through inheritance and similar means, carrying out a political or economic idea, and being unable to find another job. Types of investment: a completely new investment, expansion, renewal-modernization, quality improvement, and acquiring or becoming a partner in other businesses.
  • Establishment work: investment idea → preliminary feasibility study (economic, technical, financial, and legal studies) → preliminary project → evaluation and investment decision → preparation of the final investment project → implementation → trial production → opening and completion of the project.
  • Investment evaluation criteria (5-2): payback period (investment amount / annual net cash inflow), profit/investment ratio, net present value (NPV), internal rate of return (IRR), benefit/cost ratio (present value of net revenues / present value of net outflows); methods that take the time value of money into account and those that do not.
  • Capital (5-3): fixed capital investment expenses (setting up the facility) and working capital (the funds required for the facility to operate regularly); working cycle coefficient = annual operating time / working cycle; working capital requirement = annual expenses / working cycle coefficient. The difference between paid-up and running royalties is also covered.

Week 6 – Management

Management levels: lower level (foreman, supervisor; technical-operational), middle level (branch manager, department head; tactical-coordination), upper level (general manager, chairman of the board; strategic). Managerial roles: interpersonal, informational, and decision-making roles; skills: human relations, conceptual, technical. Management functions: planning, organizing, directing, controlling. Organizations are socio-technical open systems that are affected by the environment and affect it; the general environment and the task environment are distinguished. The concepts of vision and mission, and decision making with constraints and choices, are also in this week.

Week 7 – Planning, Organizing, and Leadership

Planning: the activity of deciding in advance what will be done, when, for what purpose, how, where, and by whom. Planning principles: timing appropriate to conditions, environmental factors, teamwork and coordination, reliance on concrete data, and flexibility. Vision is the point the organization wants to reach in the long term; mission is the organization's reason for existence; objectives are the expression of goals at the lower levels. Standing plans: policy, procedure/method, rule; single-use plans: strategy, tactic, program, budget, project. Organizing: bringing together and coordinating multiple employees to achieve a common goal; there is no one best way to organize. Comparison of mechanistic and organic systems (vertical/horizontal structure, routine tasks/empowered roles). Types of power: coercive, reward, legitimate, expert, referent (based on the acceptance of one's personality), and connection power. Career development (vertical movement, job change/rotation) is also covered.

Week 9 – Production

Production systems: one-off/project production (bridge, dam, ship), batch (serial) production, and mass (continuous) production (automobiles, cement); matching demand and supply in service production. Production management: capacity and planning, scheduling (waiting, setup, and processing times), inventory control (periodic and continuous; two-bin method; economic order quantity), quality control (acceptance sampling, statistical process control), flexible production, and automation.

Week 10 – Marketing

The shift from a production orientation to a sales and market orientation: increasing production when demand > supply, and market-based business management when supply > demand. Marketing is the whole of the activities of identifying needs, determining goods and services, pricing, distribution, and promotion; it creates time, place, and ownership utility. Topics: marketing information system and marketing research (survey, observation, experiment), market segmentation and positioning, consumer and industrial products (convenience, shopping, specialty, and unsought goods), the product–service continuum, the marketing mix (product, price, distribution, promotion), distribution channels (intensive, selective, and exclusive distribution), pricing methods (by customer, place, time, product, cost, and target price), and direct marketing.

Week 11 – Human Resources Management

Human resources management (HRM): the activities of finding and developing the right person for the right job at the right time for an organization; reactive and proactive approaches. Human resources planning (current supply: applications, skills inventory, performance and pay records; demand forecasting), job analysis and job description (job identity, job summary), recruitment and selection (interview, objectivity, validity, reliability), orientation, training methods (rotation, apprenticeship/mentoring, lecture, behavior modeling, simulation, programmed learning), performance appraisal, and compensation.

Week 12 – Public Relations

The relationships that all businesses must establish with their environments in order to exist and sustain their lives. Public relations tools: printed media, visual and audio media, other tools, and tools for obtaining information from the public.

Week 13 – Finance

Financial management: the optimal procurement and use of the funds a business needs. The concept of capital (in economics, produced means of production; in accounting, unpaid capital; working capital), tangible and intangible (goodwill, patents, concessions) capital, financing by source (equity, external sources, self-financing; cost of external sources and cost of equity), examples of the balance sheet (assets–liabilities, cash, receivables, inventory, fixed assets, trade credit, bank debt, paid-in capital) and the funds flow statement; current ratio, break-even analysis, factoring, and forfaiting.
