## CD technology and history

The CD is the standard medium on which digital data is stored in machine-readable form, and it is read by a laser-based reader; it provides faster and more accurate storage than tapes and cassettes because the data on the tracks is arranged sequentially. The thick carrier layer is mostly made of **polycarbonate**; data is stored on a reflective layer (mostly aluminum) along a spiral track as **pits** (depth approximately 120 nm) and **lands** (flat areas); pit–land transitions determine the 1s, and intervals without transitions determine the 0s. At the beginning of the disc, the **Lead-in** section holds the table of contents. The laser videodisc and the CD derive from the optical disc invented by James T. Russell; David Paul Gregg developed an analog optical disc for video recording in 1961 (1969 patents); Philips and MCA joined forces in 1975, and LaserVision was released to the market in 1980.

## Frequently asked questions about optical discs and other works

- One work answers questions such as "Will optical discs eliminate tape?" in the context of professional video recording (optical discs will be used together with tape, DVCAM and MPEG IMX will support tape in recording; they can be used for news gathering, production and post-production), and describes disc storage conditions (a lifespan of more than 30 years at 20 °C and 40% humidity) and the differences between CD-R/CD-RW and DVD±R/RW.
- The "Assignment 2" work, with a due date of 05.01.2009, describes the structure of the optical disc, the four parts of the CD-ROM drive (three of which are step motors), the difference in transfer rates between inner and outer tracks, and capacities (CD-R 700 MB; single-layer DVD around 4.7 GB; Blu-ray disc).
- Another work summarizes the history and operating principle of the optical disc; the new format developed by the Blu-ray Disc Association and future plans are also mentioned.
