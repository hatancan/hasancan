### Introduction

The purpose of a communication system is to transmit information from a point called the SOURCE to another point called the DESTINATION.  Telephone, radio, and television are the most common examples of communication systems.  Communication systems are classified according to the type of communication.  In design, various factors are considered, such as the number of sources, the number of users, the distance, whether users are stationary or mobile, and the reliability and security of the system.

### Elements of a Communication System

Source: Generates messages such as human voice, light, or heat.  Input Transducer: Converts the non-electrical input message into an electrical waveform (baseband signal).  Transmitter: Modifies the baseband signal for efficient transmission.  Transmission Medium (Channel): The medium to which the transmitter output is sent (coaxial cable, waveguide, optical fiber, or  Receiver: Processes the received signal in order to eliminate the effects of the modifications made at the transmitter and in the channel.  Output Transducer: Converts the electrical waveform into the original form needed by the user.  Destination: The unit to which the message is delivered, such as a human or a computer.

### Limitations of Communication Systems

The transmitted signal is subject to the following undesired effects caused by the transmission medium: NOISE: Random and unpredictable signals generated by natural processes outside or inside the system. External Noise Sources: Such as switches of electrical devices, automobile ignition radiation, fluorescent lights, solar radiation, and electrical storms. When necessary precautions are taken, external noise can be minimized or even eliminated. Internal Noise Sources: The thermal motion of electrons in conductors (thermal noise), and the random emission, diffusion, or recombination of charge carriers in electronic devices (shot noise). When necessary precautions are taken, internal noise can be reduced, but it can never be eliminated.

One of the most fundamental factors limiting the communication rate is the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR). As the distance from the transmitter increases, the noise level rises while the signal power decreases. Therefore, the SNR decreases continuously along the channel. Amplifying the received signal in order to counter attenuation also amplifies the noise. Moreover, in such a case, the SNR will deteriorate even further because of the noise generated by the amplifier circuit. Some special filters are used at the receiver to improve the SNR.

ATTENUATION: Defined as the decrease in signal power as the signal travels through the transmission medium. It is also called transmission loss. It is directly proportional to distance and frequency. When the atmosphere is used as the transmission medium, rain and snow also increase attenuation. Attenuation can be counteracted by using repeaters along the transmission medium and, at the receiver, by using special filters (equalizers) whose amplitude and phase characteristics are the inverse of those of the channel. DISTORTION: Defined as changes in the waveform caused by the imperfect response of the system. Distortion alters the shape of the transmitted signal. Output Signal (Distorted)

To prevent distortion, the system bandwidth should be chosen within the region where the system response characteristic is constant, and the system bandwidth should be greater than the signal bandwidth. However, it is generally not possible to satisfy these conditions in practical applications. In the case of linear distortion, the distortion can be eliminated or minimized by using an equalizer at the receiver. INTERFERENCE: Occurs due to the combination of two or more signals in the same frequency band. Interference may produce a stronger signal as a result of two signals adding together, or a weaker signal as a result of an interaction in which they reduce each other's power.

### Modulation

 It is not always possible to transmit baseband signals generated by various information sources directly over a given channel. Therefore, the baseband signal is used to vary some parameters of a high-frequency carrier signal. This process is known as MODULATION.  At the receiver, in order to recover the baseband signal, the signal undergoes a process that is the inverse of the modulation performed at the transmitter, called DEMODULATION.

Depending on the type of carrier wave, there are two types  Continuous-Wave Modulation: Here the carrier is a high-frequency sinusoid. One of the parameters of this sinusoid (amplitude, frequency, or phase) is varied in proportion to the amplitude of the baseband signal. Analog Continuous-Wave Modulation: Here the modulating signal is analog. Amplitude Modulation (AM), Frequency Modulation (FM)1 and Phase Modulation (PM)1 1: FM and PM are sometimes also referred to together as Angle Modulation.

Modulating (baseband) signal; Amplitude-modulated wave; Frequency- or phase-modulated wave

Digital Continuous-Wave Modulation: Here the modulating signal is digital, and this signal varies the amplitude, frequency, and phase of the sinusoidal carrier in proportion to its own amplitude. Depending on whether the modulating signal is binary or M-ary, there are two types of digital continuous-wave modulation.  Binary Modulation: Here, the modulating signal consists of non-return-to-zero (NRZ) rectangular pulses. The modulated parameter is keyed from one discrete value to another. In this way the following modulation methods are obtained: Amplitude Shift Keying (ASK) or On-Off Keying (OOK); Frequency Shift Keying (FSK); Phase Shift Keying (PSK) or Phase Reversal Keying (PRK)

Digitally Modulated Waveforms; (a) Baseband signal

Other binary modulation types are: Differential PSK (DPSK); Minimum Shift Keying (MSK), also known as Fast FSK or Continuous-Phase PSK  Quadrature-Carrier Modulation: A modulation that uses two carriers in phase quadrature. Quadrature Amplitude Modulation (QAM) is a typical example of this modulation type.  M-ary Modulation: A modulation type that uses M symbols for communication. The M symbols are one of the following: M different amplitude levels (multi-amplitude signaling); M different phases (multi-phase signaling); a combination of M different amplitudes and phases (M-ary APK)

The amplitude, width, and position of the pulses are varied in proportion to the amplitude of the baseband signal, yielding the following modulation types: Pulse Amplitude Modulation (PAM); Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) or Pulse Duration Modulation (PDM); Pulse Position Modulation (PPM). By appropriately encoding the PAM signal:  Pulse Code Modulation (PCM)  Differential Pulse Code Modulation (DPCM)  Adaptive Delta Modulation (ADM)

### The Necessity of Modulation

Modulation Provides Efficient Radiation: For electromagnetic energy to be radiated efficiently, the dimensions of the transmitting antenna must be on the order of one-tenth of the wavelength of the radiated signal or larger. Considering the equation λ=c/f (c=3x10^8 m/s), it can be seen that the antenna size decreases as the carrier frequency increases. For example, the wavelength of a voice signal in the band between f = 100 Hz and 3000 Hz is between λ = 3000 km and 100 km. If this voice signal is transmitted by modulating a carrier of f = 100 MHz, the wavelength drops to λ = 3 m. For this reason, modulation is needed in order to shift the frequency components of the baseband signal to the frequency of the carrier signal.

Modulation Enables Simultaneous Transmission: Modulation is necessary so that many baseband signals can be transmitted simultaneously over the same transmission medium. The following methods are used for simultaneous transmission: Frequency-Division Multiplexing (FDM): Different carrier frequencies are used to modulate each baseband signal. In this method, the carrier frequencies are chosen far enough apart to prevent the spectra of the modulated signals from overlapping and interfering with each other. At the receiver, tunable bandpass filters are used to select the desired station or signal. In this way, the channel bandwidth is shared among various signals without any overlap.

Time-Division Multiplexing (TDM): Baseband signals are converted into PAM signals consisting of narrow pulses. The spacing between the pulses of one signal is used for the pulses of other signals. In this way, the pulses of various signals are interleaved, and the transmission time is shared among a certain number of signals. At the receiver, the pulse trains are separated from one another according to the order in which they were placed. Modulation Reduces the Effects of Noise and Interference: Frequency and phase modulation make it possible to control the transmission bandwidth and the SNR.

### Coding

Coding is a symbol-processing operation required for reliable digital transmission. Encoding converts the digital message into a new sequence of symbols, and decoding converts the encoded sequence back to its original form (the message). During this conversion, a few errors, possibly caused by data corruption during transmission, may also be corrected. Error-control codes are used to eliminate the effects of errors that may occur due to channel noise or other imperfections. By adding extra "check bits" to each binary codeword, it is possible to detect and even correct the vast majority of errors. In fact, with this method redundancy is added to the message and the signal bandwidth is increased, but this also increases communication reliability. Example: If a 1 is appended to the codeword 0001 to make the number of 1s even, the new codeword becomes 00011. The decoder at the receiver checks whether the number of ones is even. If this number is not even, the receiver recognizes that an error occurred during transmission and requests that the message be retransmitted. This method is known as even parity checking.

### Advantages of Digital Communication over Analog

Communication: Digital communication is more robust against channel noise and distortion. Repeaters used along the transmission path detect the digital signal and retransmit it free of noise. In this way, the accumulation of noise along the transmission path is prevented. This is not possible in analog communication. Digital circuit implementations are flexible and allow the use of microprocessors, digital switching, and large-scale integrated circuits. Digital signals can be transmitted with a very low error rate and high accuracy, and Multiplexing of digital signals is quite easy and more efficient. Digital communication is far superior to analog communication in terms of controlling bandwidth and SNR. For all of the above reasons, digital communication is generally preferred over analog communication.

### Continuous-Time and Discrete-Time Signals

 Signals specified at every instant of time are defined as continuous-time signals.  Signals specified only at certain instants of time are defined as discrete-time signals. Continuous-Time Signal; Discrete-Time Signal

 A discrete-time signal can be obtained by sampling a continuous-time signal.  In some cases it is also necessary to return from the discrete-time signal to the continuous-time signal.  The continuous-time signal can be recovered after sampling carried out in accordance with the Sampling Theorem.  The sampling theorem states the following: If the highest frequency in the spectrum of the signal is B, it is possible to reconstruct the signal using samples taken from it at a rate of no fewer than 2B samples per second.

### Analog and Digital Signals

 Signals whose amplitude values are taken from a continuous range are called analog signals.  The concepts of analog and digital signals are not the same as those of continuous-time and discrete-time signals.  This is because a signal can be digital and continuous-time, as well as analog and discrete-time.

 It is possible to obtain a digital signal from an analog signal by using a quantizer.  The amplitude of the analog signal is divided into L intervals.  Each sample value is rounded to the value at the midpoint of the interval in which the original value lies.  Quantization is a lossy process. Note: Therefore, a digital discrete-time signal is obtained by sampling and quantizing an analog continuous-time signal. (Encoding is assumed to be performed as well)

### Deterministic and Random Signals

 A signal whose physical description is completely known is called a deterministic signal.  A signal described only by its statistical properties is called a random signal.

### Energy and Power Signals

The energy of a real signal f (t ) is defined as E f. For a complex-valued signal, If E f < ∞, then the signal f (t ) is an energy signal. Note! The average power of energy signals is zero!

The necessary condition for the energy to be finite is that the signal amplitude goes to zero as time goes to infinity. When dealing with signals of infinite energy (for example periodic signals), it is more meaningful to deal with the power of the signal rather than its energy. Power signals physically continue forever or suddenly take an infinite value. If a signal satisfies the condition, it is said to be a power signal.  A signal cannot be both an energy signal and a power signal at the same time.
